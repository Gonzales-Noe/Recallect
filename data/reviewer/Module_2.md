# Module 2: Ethics for IT Workers and IT Users

## Learning Objectives
- What relationships must an IT worker manage, and what key ethical issues can arise in each?
- What can be done to encourage the professionalism of IT workers?
- What ethical issues do IT users face, and what can be done to encourage their ethical behavior?

## Opening Case: Why This Matters
Queensland, Australia awarded IBM a payroll system contract initially estimated at $5 million. The
project went badly wrong — checks were late, wrong, or missing — and costs ballooned past $1
billion. An investigation alleged IBM misrepresented its expertise and true costs during bidding,
while IBM blamed poor project management by Queensland. This shows how fragile trust is in IT
outsourcing, and why clear ethical expectations between client and vendor matter from day one.

## Overview: the Six Key IT Worker Relationships
An ethical IT worker must manage relationships with: (1) employers, (2) clients, (3) suppliers,
(4) other professionals, (5) IT users, and (6) society at large. Each relationship carries its own
set of ethical risks.

## 1. IT Workers and Employers
The basics of this relationship (job title, pay, hours, benefits) are usually agreed on before hiring;
other details (like company policy on trade secrets or software use) develop over time or are
covered by a code of conduct.
- Software Piracy: IT staff sometimes enable piracy — either allowing it or engaging in it directly —
  often to cut IT spending. Groups like SIIA (Software & Information Industry Association) and BSA
  (The Software Alliance) exist to fight this. North America has the lowest regional piracy rate at
  about 17%, still representing roughly $10 billion in lost revenue; the global rate is around 43%
  (about $62.7 billion in commercial value lost).
- Trade Secrets: Confidential, high-value information a company protects (e.g., KFC's spice recipe,
  the Coca-Cola formula, a chip manufacturing process). Employers require confidentiality
  agreements because leaving employees might reveal secrets to competitors.
  Example: Zillow hired a rival's chief strategy officer, who allegedly copied and deleted files before
  leaving. Zillow eventually paid $130 million to settle the trade-secret theft lawsuit.
- Whistle-Blowing: An employee's effort to expose a negligent, illegal, unethical, abusive, or
  dangerous act that threatens the public interest — usually after internal channels are ignored.
  It can carry real personal risk (retaliation, firing).
  Example: A former Oracle employee alleged she was fired after refusing management pressure to
  inflate cloud revenue accruals; Oracle's stock dropped 4% after the lawsuit became public.

## 2. IT Workers and Clients
Each side agrees to provide value: the IT worker delivers hardware, software, or services; the
client provides compensation, access, and information. This relationship is usually built on trust
despite an expertise gap — the client often can't fully judge the technical work, so must rely on
the IT worker's honesty.
- Misrepresentation happens when a party gives a false impression of facts, its expertise, or a
  product's cost/quality.
- Breach of Contract occurs when a party fails to meet contract terms; a material breach impairs
  the very essence of the deal, letting the harmed party either cancel the contract and sue for
  damages, or continue the contract and sue for losses.
  Example: HP Enterprise won $3 billion in damages after Oracle dropped support for its database
  software on HP's Itanium-based servers, a move the jury found was a breach of contract intended
  to boost Oracle's own competing hardware.
- Common causes of IT project trouble:
  - Scope creep — uncontrolled changes to requirements causing cost/time overruns.
  - Poor communication — leads to a system that doesn't meet expectations.
  - Delivery of an obsolete solution — competitor tech overtakes what was delivered.
  - Legacy systems — undisclosed old systems make integration far harder than planned.

## 3. IT Workers and Suppliers
Good supplier relationships come from fair dealing, not aggressive demands.
- Bribery: giving money, property, or favors to gain a business advantage (often called a "kickback").
  Both the giver and the taker commit a crime.
  Example: Five former Foxconn employees, including two senior managers, were charged with
  accepting kickbacks from suppliers in exchange for contracts and help passing quality checks.
- Bribes vs. Gifts: bribes are secret, often given through a third party, and create an obligation to
  favor the giver. Gifts are open, given directly, and expect nothing in return. The safest rule: if a
  gift must be hidden, treat it as a bribe. Most companies require gifts to be declared, and reject
  anything beyond token value.
- Internal Control: the system of people, policies, processes, and procedures that gives reasonable
  assurance that operations are efficient, financial reporting is reliable, and laws are followed. A key
  principle is separation of duties — splitting tasks (like approving payments vs. depositing cash)
  among different people so fraud requires collusion, not just one bad actor.
- Foreign Corrupt Practices Act (FCPA): makes it a crime for U.S. citizens or companies (or firms
  listed on U.S. exchanges) to bribe foreign officials. Penalties can reach $2 million per violation for
  companies and 5 years in prison plus $100,000 fines for individuals.
  Example: Hewlett-Packard paid $108 million to settle FCPA violations after subsidiaries in Mexico,
  Poland, and Russia allegedly bribed officials using slush funds and secret bookkeeping.

## 4. IT Workers and Other Professionals
Professionals owe each other loyalty and should avoid public criticism of peers, since how the
profession is perceived affects everyone in it.
- Résumé Inflation: exaggerating or lying about skills/credentials. Roughly 30% of applicants
  exaggerate accomplishments; about 10% seriously misrepresent their background. Common areas
  of exaggeration: skills, job responsibilities, employment dates, job titles, and degrees.
  Example: Yahoo's new CEO Scott Thompson resigned within a year after discrepancies were found
  in the academic credentials listed on his résumé.
- Inappropriate Sharing of Corporate Information: IT workers often have access to sensitive
  databases; leaking this information (even informally) can be grounds for termination or criminal
  charges.
  Example: A former Ofcom contractor gave a rival broadcaster (UKTV) six years of competitors'
  confidential financial data; the employee was fired once discovered.

## 5. IT Workers and IT Users
IT users rely on IT workers to understand their needs and deliver products responsibly within
budget and time constraints. IT workers also have a duty to build an environment where ethical
use of IT is the norm — discouraging piracy, misuse of resources, and improper information
sharing among users (this connects directly to the IT-user issues covered later in this module).

## 6. IT Workers and Society
Regulatory laws can't cover every possible risk, so IT professionals are expected to foresee and
prevent harm their work could cause. Example: a systems analyst designing a control system for a
chemical plant must consider the safety of workers and nearby residents, not just meet the spec.
This is why professional codes of ethics (below) matter — they fill the gap that formal law can't.

## Encouraging the Professionalism of IT Workers
A professional has the skill, judgment, and habits expected of someone trained and experienced.
IT workers can strengthen the profession's reputation through 4 actions:
1. Subscribing to a professional code of ethics
2. Joining and participating in professional organizations
3. Obtaining appropriate certifications
4. Supporting government licensing where available

## Professional Codes of Ethics
A written statement of an occupation's principles and core values. Most codes have two parts:
what the organization aspires to be, and specific rules members must follow. Benefits include:
- Ethical decision making guided by shared values.
- High standards of practice, reinforced through the code's expectations.
- Trust and respect from the public, since people rely on professionals to act with integrity.
- An evaluation benchmark professionals (and their peers) can use for self-assessment.

## Professional Organizations
No single IT organization is universally dominant, but several offer valuable codes of ethics:
- ACM (Association for Computing Machinery) — founded 1947, the world's largest computing
  society, with over 100,000 members worldwide.
- IEEE-CS (IEEE Computer Society) — founded 1946, about 60,000 members, one of the oldest and
  largest IT professional groups.
- AITP (Association of Information Technology Professionals) — traces back to 1951, provides
  seminars, networking, and its own code of ethics/standards of conduct.
- SANS Institute — focused on information security training and certification; publishes free
  security research and runs the Internet Storm Center to track malicious online activity.
The ACM and IEEE-CS jointly developed the Software Engineering Code of Ethics and Professional
Practice, built around 8 principles: Public, Client and Employer, Product, Judgment, Management,
Profession, Colleagues, and Self (lifelong learning).

## Certification vs. Licensing
- Certification indicates a specific skill set, in the opinion of the certifying body. It's usually
  voluntary and can apply to products too (e.g., "Wi-Fi CERTIFIED"). Common examples: CompTIA
  Security+, PMP (Project Management Professional), vendor certs from Cisco, Microsoft, Oracle, etc.
- Licensing is a government-issued permission to practice, generally required for professions where
  incompetence could harm public health or safety (doctors, lawyers, CPAs). As of recent data, about
  40 U.S. states support licensing for software engineers, requiring accredited education, years of
  supervised experience, and passing exams.
- Debate: proponents argue licensing would raise IT standards and create real accountability;
  without it, there's no clear "professional malpractice" standard for most IT workers.

## IT Professional Malpractice — Key Legal Concepts
- Negligence: failing to do what a reasonable person would do, or doing what a reasonable person
  would not do.
- Duty of Care: the obligation to protect others from unreasonable harm or risk.
- Reasonable Person / Reasonable Professional Standard: courts judge ordinary people against how
  an objective, careful person would act — but judge licensed experts against a higher, professional
  standard (a licensed database administrator is held to a higher bar than an unlicensed analyst).
- Breach of Duty of Care: failing to act as a reasonable person would, whether through action or
  inaction.
- Professional Malpractice: liability that results when a professional breaches their duty of care and
  causes injury or harm — historically hard to apply to software engineers because, until recently,
  there was no licensed, uniform professional standard to measure them against.

## Ethical Issues Faced By IT Users
"IT user" refers to anyone who uses hardware/software (as opposed to those who build or maintain
it). Common ethical issues:
- Software Piracy: often happens when employees copy work software for home use without an
  additional license. Mobile app piracy is especially high — some popular Android games have
  piracy rates exceeding 90%.
- Inappropriate Use of Computing Resources: browsing unrelated websites, gaming, or viewing
  inappropriate content at work — wastes productivity and can create legal exposure (e.g.,
  harassment claims from an inappropriate workplace environment).
- Inappropriate Sharing of Information: sharing private data (employee/customer records) or
  confidential company data (plans, formulas, strategy) with unauthorized parties, even accidentally.
  Examples: the 2010 WikiLeaks leak of State Department documents by a low-level Army IT user;
  a Morgan Stanley adviser fired (and later fined) for stealing and posting almost 350,000 clients'
  account data.

## Supporting the Ethical Practices of IT Users
Organizations can encourage ethical IT use through several tools:
- Clear Guidelines for Hardware/Software Use — ensure employees have legal software whether
  working in the office, on the road, or at home.
- Acceptable Use Policy (AUP): a document that lays out rules users must accept to use company
  computing/network resources. An effective AUP includes 5 key elements:
  1. Purpose — why the policy exists and what it aims to achieve
  2. Scope — who and what is covered
  3. Policy — what counts as acceptable vs. unacceptable use, with examples
  4. Compliance — who monitors it and how
  5. Sanctions — consequences for violations
  The organization's information security (infosec) group is typically responsible for monitoring
  AUP compliance.
- Structuring Information Systems — limiting data access to only what each role needs (a sales
  manager shouldn't automatically see R&D or payroll data).
- Firewalls — hardware/software that forms the first line of defense between a company network
  and the internet.
- Compliance — acting in accordance with laws/policies such as Sarbanes-Oxley (financial reporting
  controls) or HIPAA (healthcare data privacy). Non-compliance can bring lawsuits or government
  fines.
- Audit Committees and Internal Audit Teams — the audit committee (part of the board) oversees
  financial reporting integrity, legal compliance, and the independent auditor's performance. The
  internal audit team checks that internal controls are working, verifies assets are safeguarded, and
  measures compliance with company policy.
  Example: Marvell Technology's audit committee uncovered pressured "early revenue booking"
  practices; the CEO and president were subsequently fired.
