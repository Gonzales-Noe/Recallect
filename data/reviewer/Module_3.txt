MODULE 3
CYBERATTACKS AND CYBERSECURITY

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- Why are computer incidents so prevalent, and what are their effects?
- What can be done to implement a strong security program to prevent cyberattacks?
- What actions must be taken in the event of a successful security intrusion?

OPENING CASE: WHY THIS MATTERS
A zero-day exploit is a cyberattack that happens before software developers even know about (or
have fixed) a security flaw. Information about a single zero-day vulnerability in Apple's iOS was
reportedly sold on the black market for $500,000, and U.S. government contractors reportedly
pay millions per year for packages of zero-day exploits. Sometimes agencies keep vulnerabilities
secret to use them for their own intelligence purposes rather than warning the public — for
example, the FBI refused to disclose the method it used to unlock the San Bernardino shooter's
iPhone. This tension — between fixing security holes and exploiting them — sits at the heart of
modern cybersecurity ethics.

WHY COMPUTER INCIDENTS ARE SO PREVALENT
Several trends have combined to sharply increase the number, variety, and severity of security
incidents:
- Increasing Complexity: modern computing environments (cloud, mobile, networks, virtualization)
  are enormously interconnected, and every new connected device is another possible entry point
  for attackers.
- Expanding and Changing Systems: businesses have moved from isolated mainframes to globally
  networked systems, making it hard for IT teams to keep up with new risks.
- BYOD (Bring Your Own Device): letting employees use personal phones/laptops for work boosts
  productivity, but personal devices are also used for risky non-work activity (browsing, shopping)
  and often lack basic protections like passcodes — creating an easy path for malware to enter the
  company network.
- Reliance on Software with Known Vulnerabilities: an exploit takes advantage of a specific system
  weakness. Developers issue patches to fix these, but delays in installing patches (often due to
  workload trade-offs) leave systems exposed. Roughly 6,000+ new software vulnerabilities are
  identified annually.
- Increasing Sophistication of Attackers: today's threat isn't just a lone "geek hacker" — it's often
  an organized group (e.g., Anonymous, nation-state-sponsored teams) with real funding, tools, and
  strategic targets.

TYPES OF EXPLOITS (COMMON ATTACK METHODS)
- Ransomware: locks a user out of their data/system until a demand (usually payment) is met.
  Example: Hollywood Presbyterian Medical Center paid a $12,000 ransom in 2016 after hackers
  encrypted hospital data, forcing staff back onto paper and fax machines for a week.
- Virus: malicious code disguised as something else, that runs when an infected file is opened, and
  spreads through user action (sharing files, opening infected email attachments). A macro virus
  uses application scripting languages (like VBScript) to infect documents and templates.
- Worm: unlike a virus, a worm lives in active memory and spreads itself automatically — no user
  action required — often emailing copies of itself to everyone in a victim's contact list. Worms like
  Code Red, SirCam, and Melissa each caused over $1 billion in damage; Conficker, Storm, and
  ILOVEYOU together caused over $5 billion.
- Trojan Horse: a seemingly harmless program that hides malicious code, often disguised as a
  software update or game. Can create a "backdoor" giving attackers ongoing access.
  A logic bomb is a type of Trojan that triggers on a specific event (a date, a keystroke sequence).
  Example: DHS believes Russia planted Trojan malware in U.S. critical infrastructure (pipelines,
  power grids, even nuclear plants) as early as 2011, as a deterrent against U.S. cyberattacks.
- Blended Threat: combines a virus, worm, Trojan, and other malicious code into one sophisticated
  payload that spreads through multiple channels at once (email, chat, file-sharing).
- Spam: unsolicited bulk email, often advertising, sometimes carrying malware links. The CAN-SPAM
  Act makes spam legal only if it doesn't use a fake return address, is labeled as an ad, and offers an
  opt-out. CAPTCHA (Completely Automated Public Turing Test to Tell Computers and Humans Apart)
  helps stop bots from mass-registering free email accounts to send spam.
- DDoS (Distributed Denial-of-Service) Attack: floods a target with traffic from many hijacked
  computers (a botnet of "zombie" machines) until legitimate users can't get through. It doesn't
  actually break into the target system — it overwhelms it.
  Example: A massive 2016 DDoS attack on Dyn (a DNS provider) took down access to Airbnb,
  Amazon, Twitter, PayPal, and other major sites for millions of East Coast users.
- Rootkit: software that gives an attacker hidden, administrator-level access without the user's
  knowledge. Once installed, it's very hard to detect and usually requires a full system reformat to
  remove.
- Advanced Persistent Threat (APT): a stealthy, long-term intrusion aimed at stealing data over
  weeks or months, unfolding in 5 phases: Reconnaissance → Incursion → Discovery → Capture →
  Export. Example: the hacker group Carbanak used APT techniques (starting with spear phishing) to
  steal over $1 billion from banks across several countries.
- Phishing: fraudulent emails that trick recipients into revealing personal data or downloading
  malware, often by impersonating a trusted brand (banks, credit card companies). An estimated
  156 million phishing emails go out daily.
  Spear Phishing targets a specific organization's employees, often spoofing an executive's identity
  to pressure staff into compliance (e.g., 2016 attacks that stole employee tax records by
  impersonating CEOs/CFOs).
- Smishing and Vishing: phishing via text message (smishing) or voicemail/phone call (vishing),
  usually claiming there's a problem with a bank account or card that needs "immediate action."
- Cyberespionage: malware deployed to secretly steal sensitive data (product plans, designs, client
  data) from governments, militaries, or corporations for competitive or strategic advantage.
- Cyberterrorism: using IT to intimidate governments or civilians by disabling critical infrastructure
  (energy, transportation, finance, emergency response) for political, religious, or ideological goals.
  Example: In 2015, cyberterrorists caused a 3-hour power outage for 80,000 customers in Ukraine,
  and also jammed the utility's phone lines so customers couldn't even report the outage.

FEDERAL LAWS FOR PROSECUTING COMPUTER CRIME

| Law                                                | Focus                                                              |
|-----------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Computer Fraud and Abuse Act                        | Unauthorized access, transmitting harmful code, trafficking passwords |
| Fraud and Related Activity in Connection with Access Devices Statute | False claims about unauthorized credit card use            |
| Stored Wire and Electronic Communications Act        | Unlawful access to stored electronic communications                |
| USA Patriot Act                                     | Defines cyberterrorism and its penalties (5-20 years in prison)     |
| Sarbanes-Oxley Act                                  | Protects shareholders/public from accounting fraud                 |
| HIPAA                                               | Regulates use/disclosure of health information                     |
| Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act                              | Protects consumers' nonpublic financial information                |
| Federal Information Security Management Act (FISMA) | Requires federal agencies to secure their data/systems              |

Under the USA Patriot Act, cyberterrorism includes any unauthorized hacking that causes over
$5,000 in aggregate losses — a threshold that's surprisingly easy to hit, meaning even minor "pranks"
can technically qualify someone for very serious charges.

THE CIA SECURITY TRIAD
The foundation of nearly all IT security strategy:
- Confidentiality: only authorized people can access sensitive data.
- Integrity: data can only be changed by authorized people, keeping it accurate and trustworthy.
- Availability: data/systems can be accessed whenever needed, including during disaster recovery.
  ("Five 9s," or 99.999% availability, means less than one hour of downtime per year.)
No system can be 100% secure, so organizations use layered security — multiple defensive layers
so that breaking through one still leaves an attacker facing more obstacles.

IMPLEMENTING SECURITY AT 4 LEVELS

1. ORGANIZATION LEVEL
- Security Strategy: a risk-based plan with active governance to limit damage from incidents and
  keep the business running.
- Risk Assessment: an 8-step process — identify key assets, identify possible threats, estimate
  likelihood, estimate impact, decide how to mitigate each threat, check feasibility, run a cost-
  benefit analysis, and decide whether to implement a countermeasure. The idea of "reasonable
  assurance" means spending on security should never exceed the actual value it protects.
- Disaster Recovery Plan: a documented process for recovering hardware, software, data, networks,
  and facilities after a disaster (natural or manmade). Mission-critical processes get priority recovery
  because their failure threatens the business's survival (e.g., a billing system going down).
- Security Dashboard: software that tracks key security metrics (patch backlogs, failed logins,
  audit results) in one place so problems get spotted and fixed faster.

2. NETWORK LEVEL
- Authentication Methods: usernames/passwords, smart cards + PINs, or biometrics (fingerprint,
  retina scan). Two-factor authentication combines two of: something you know, something you
  have, something you are.
- Firewall: hardware/software standing guard between an internal network and the internet,
  blocking traffic that isn't explicitly permitted. A next-generation firewall (NGFW) goes further,
  inspecting actual packet content for hidden threats.
- Router: connects networks and can restrict access using passphrases or MAC address filtering.
- Encryption: scrambles data (plaintext → ciphertext) so only someone with the right key can read
  it. Symmetric encryption uses one key for both encrypting and decrypting; asymmetric uses a
  different key for each. Longer keys mean stronger protection (e.g., 128-bit is stronger than 56-bit).
  TLS (Transport Layer Security) protects online transactions by encrypting communication between
  a browser and a server after a "handshake" exchange.
- Proxy Servers & VPNs: a proxy server hides your identity from websites you visit by acting as a
  middleman; a VPN (Virtual Private Network) securely encrypts a remote user's entire connection
  to an organization's network, protecting data even on public Wi-Fi.
- Intrusion Detection System (IDS): monitors network traffic for signs of an attack. Knowledge-
  based IDS watches for known attack patterns; behavior-based IDS flags unusual activity that
  deviates from a normal-behavior model.

3. APPLICATION LEVEL
- Authentication Methods: single-factor (just a password) vs. two-factor (password + something
  you have or are).
- User Roles and Accounts: giving each user only the access they need for their specific job — an
  accountant shouldn't see HR records, and vice versa.
- Data Encryption: protecting sensitive data inside major systems (ERP, CRM, etc.), whether stored
  on-site, in the cloud, or with a third party.

4. END-USER LEVEL (often the weakest link)
- Security Education: teaching users to guard passwords, avoid suspicious links, and report unusual
  activity — because human error is one of the biggest security risks of all.
- Authentication Methods: passcodes, Touch ID/fingerprint locks on personal devices.
- Antivirus Software: scans for known virus "signatures" and can clean, delete, or quarantine
  infected files. Must be updated constantly, since most attacks use already-known malware.
- Data Encryption: full-disk encryption protects data even if a device itself is physically stolen.

RESPONSE TO A CYBERATTACK
No security system is perfect, so every organization needs a response plan, approved in advance
by both legal and senior management, that addresses:
- Incident Notification: deciding who inside and outside the company needs to know, and when —
  including whether/when to involve the FBI or notify affected customers. Many laws now require
  disclosure to customers when their personal data is breached, precisely because silence is
  considered unethical.
- Protection of Evidence and Activity Logs: documenting everything (what happened, when, who
  was involved) both to support recovery and to preserve potential court evidence.
- Incident Containment: acting fast to stop the damage from spreading — deciding quickly whether
  systems need to be shut down or disconnected.
- Eradication: removing the malware/threat, verifying backups are clean, and creating a new,
  verified-safe backup before restoring operations.
- Incident Follow-Up: a formal incident report covering what happened, how, the damage caused,
  and lessons learned — used to update the response plan and prevent a repeat.
Many organizations outsource ongoing protection to a Managed Security Service Provider (MSSP)
— a company that monitors and manages network security on their behalf, since in-house expertise
can be costly to maintain (especially for small/mid-sized organizations).

COMPUTER FORENSICS
A discipline combining law and computer science to identify, collect, examine, and preserve digital
evidence in a way that holds up in court. Investigations may follow a crime, a lawsuit, a data loss
incident, or suspected industrial espionage. Relevant certifications include CCE (Certified Computer
Examiner), CISSP, CSFA, and GCFA — proper training strengthens an investigator's credibility as an
expert witness in legal proceedings.
