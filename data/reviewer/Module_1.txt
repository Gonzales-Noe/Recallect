AN OVERVIEW OF ETHICS

LEARNING OBJECTIVES
- What is ethics?
- What trends have increased the likelihood of unethical behavior?
- What is corporate social responsibility, and why is fostering good business ethics important?
- What measures can organizations take to improve their business ethics?
- How can you include ethical considerations in your decision making?
- What trends have increased the risk that information technology will be used in an unethical manner?

OPENING CASE: WHY THIS MATTERS
Between 2014-2015, the FCC fined major U.S. phone carriers hundreds of millions of dollars for
"cramming" — quietly adding unauthorized charges (ring tones, horoscope texts, etc.) to customer
bills. AT&T Mobility alone was fined $205 million across two cases. The people who built and ran
these billing schemes were once ordinary new hires. This shows how easily ordinary employees can
slide into unethical practices when a company culture normalizes it — and sets the stage for why
ethics needs to be studied deliberately, not assumed.

WHAT IS ETHICS?
Ethics is a code of behavior defined by the group a person belongs to (a profession, a company,
a society). It:
- Follows generally accepted norms that can change as society's needs evolve.
- Helps members of a group understand their roles and responsibilities.
- Allows people to work together toward mutual benefits such as security, resources, and shared goals.

WHAT ARE MORALS?
Morals are an individual's personal principles for deciding right from wrong. They come from a
person's core beliefs and shape how they act.
Example: Someone raised to believe honesty matters will refuse to lie even when lying is easier.
Morals get tested in complex adult situations: Is it okay to lie to spare someone's feelings? Should
you report a coworker's substance abuse? Can you exaggerate your résumé?

VIRTUES & VICES
- Virtue: a habit that inclines someone to do what is acceptable (fairness, generosity, loyalty).
- Vice: a habit of unacceptable behavior (vanity, greed, envy, anger).
Together, a person's virtues and vices form their personal value system — the framework of morals
they live by. Note that "right" and "wrong" aren't always agreed upon everywhere: attitudes on
software piracy, for example, range from strong opposition to casual acceptance, and the global
piracy rate is estimated around 40%+ (much higher in regions where legitimate software is
unaffordable).

RELATIONSHIP OF ETHICS AND MORALS
Everything begins with core personal beliefs — the deepest values that define who you are. These
shape your individual morals (your compass for right and wrong). Your morals then interact with a
group's code of ethics — the external rules and expectations set by society or your workplace. This
whole system keeps getting reshaped by life influences: culture, education, age, traditions,
religion, gender, and personal experience.
Sometimes your group's ethics conflict with your personal morals. Example: a defense attorney is
ethically required to defend a client they personally believe is guilty of a horrible crime; a doctor's
professional ethics forbid euthanasia even if the doctor personally believes a suffering patient has
that right.

IMPORTANCE OF INTEGRITY
A person with integrity acts consistently according to their own code of principles, and extends to
others the same respect they expect for themselves.
Integrity is hard to maintain when principles conflict — for example, wanting to follow your
employer's instructions while also believing you deserve fair pay. If your employer tells you not to
report overtime hours to save budget, you can't fully honor both principles at once.
Many ethical dilemmas aren't "right vs. wrong" — they are "right vs. right." (Example: protecting
wildlife vs. securing more domestic energy sources — both can be considered morally justifiable
goals that conflict.)

MORALS vs. ETHICS vs. LAW
- Morals: an individual's personal beliefs about right and wrong, shaped by experience, culture,
  religion, education, and age.
- Ethics: shared principles or standards that guide how a person or group should behave —
  often written into a professional code of ethics.
- Law: official rules created and enforced by government institutions (police, courts,
  legislatures). Breaking a law can mean fines or imprisonment. Laws change over time and can
  even conflict across jurisdictions.
Important: being LEGAL does not automatically mean something is ETHICAL, and something ILLEGAL
isn't always considered immoral by everyone (e.g., using marijuana medically for chemotherapy
nausea is illegal in some places but seen as morally acceptable by many).

LEGAL vs. ETHICAL MATRIX

|            | Illegal                                                        | Legal                                                     |
|------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| Ethical    | Against the law, but many consider it the right thing to do     | Allowed by law and widely seen as morally right             |
| Unethical  | Against the law and considered wrong                             | Allowed by law but still seen as wrong by many               |

WHY BUSINESS ETHICS MATTERS NOW
Ethics has moved to the top of the corporate agenda because the risks of bad behavior have grown
— both in likelihood and in how damaging the fallout can be.
- Personal consequences: Executives such as Bernard Madoff (an estimated $65 billion Ponzi
  scheme) and Stewart Parnell (28 years in prison for knowingly shipping salmonella-tainted
  peanut products that killed 9 people) show that ethics failures can end careers and lives, not
  just companies.
- Costly real-world failures: Bank of America, Citigroup, Lehman Brothers, and AIG all collapsed or
  needed government bailouts tied to unwise or unethical lending practices.

ROOT CAUSES: TWO TRENDS FUELING UNETHICAL BEHAVIOR
1. Globalization — Diverse cultures and legal systems make one consistent code of ethics hard to
   apply everywhere. Example: workers at Apple's Pegatron factory faced excessive overtime for
   lower pay than domestic standards would allow.
2. Economic pressure — Tough economic climates tempt companies to bend rules to protect profit.
   Example: Tesco overstated its 2013 half-year profit by roughly $400 million.
A third factor worth noting: powerful, highly successful people sometimes come to believe their
success gives them the right to manipulate outcomes, making them more likely to cross ethical lines.

REAL-WORLD CORPORATE SCANDALS
- Enron: Hid debts and losses off its financial statements; stock collapsed from about $90 to $1,
  and the company went bankrupt in 2001.
- Volkswagen: Cheated emissions tests on roughly 11 million vehicles worldwide.
- Toshiba: Overstated earnings by about $1.2 billion over seven years, crashing its stock price.
- Amazon: Criticized for a high-pressure work culture linked to unusually high employee turnover.
- AT&T Mobility / T-Mobile / Verizon / Sprint: Fined hundreds of millions for "cramming" hidden
  charges onto customer bills (see opening case above).

INSIDE THE WORKPLACE: MOST COMMON MISCONDUCT
- Misuse of company time is the single most commonly reported offense — roughly 1 in 3 employees
  say it's the top issue they see.
- About 60% of workplace misconduct is committed by managers — the very people meant to model
  good behavior. Non-managers account for about 40%, meaning leadership sets the tone far more
  than any single policy document can.

CORPORATE SOCIAL RESPONSIBILITY (CSR)
CSR is the idea that an organization should act ethically by taking responsibility for its impact on
everyone it touches: employees, shareholders, consumers, community, environment, and suppliers.
Think of it as a company's way of "giving back" to everyone who helps it succeed.
Critical thinking check: Can a generous company still act irresponsibly? Suppose a computer
supplier donates laptops to a school but regularly misleads customers about product defects — is
that company socially responsible? CSR requires looking at BOTH the charitable side AND the
everyday honesty of daily operations, not just the donations.

SUPPLY CHAIN SUSTAINABILITY
A component of CSR: developing and maintaining a supply chain that meets today's needs without
compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs. A company's ethical responsibility doesn't
stop at its own walls — it extends to partners, contractors, manufacturers, transportation providers,
and suppliers. Key focus areas:
- Fair Labor Practices — decent treatment and working conditions throughout the chain.
- Energy and Resource Conservation — minimizing unnecessary use of energy and natural resources.
- Human Rights — ensuring suppliers and partners respect fundamental rights.
- Community Responsibility — considering the impact of operations on surrounding communities.
Example: Many IT hardware manufacturers prioritize supply-chain sustainability partly to comply
with EU regulations (like the Restriction of Hazardous Substances Directive), and because it can
also cut costs — Dell's global electronics take-back program has collected over 1 billion pounds of
e-waste since 2007.

WHY CSR AND GOOD ETHICS MATTER: 5 REASONS
1. Gaining the Goodwill of the Community — builds trust and positive relationships with customers,
   employees, and the public; philanthropy is one visible way to demonstrate this.
2. Creating an Organization That Operates Consistently — clear values help employees understand
   what's expected of them and how to treat stakeholders, while stakeholders know what to expect
   in return.
3. Fostering Good Business Practices —
   - Better employee relations: lower turnover, higher morale, greater commitment.
   - Stronger business partnerships: suppliers prefer working with fair, ethical companies.
   - The effects of bad ethics: when employees see a gap between their personal values and the
     company's actions, it can cause low morale, poor performance, and disengagement — proving
     that ethics shapes internal culture as much as external reputation.
4. Protecting the Organization and Employees from Legal Action —
   - Respondeat Superior ("let the master answer"): a legal doctrine, confirmed in the 1909 case
     United States v. New York Central & Hudson River Railroad Co., holding that an employer can be
     liable for an employee's actions even if those actions went against company policy.
   - Example: Wells Fargo employees, pressured by aggressive sales targets, created millions of
     unauthorized customer accounts. The fallout included financial penalties, customer restitution,
     and severe reputational damage — showing how unrealistic performance targets can quietly
     produce unethical behavior.
5. Avoiding Unfavorable Publicity — a company's ethical image affects customer trust, investor
   confidence, partnerships, and government oversight; bad publicity from unethical behavior can
   spread and damage a brand very quickly.

HOW ORGANIZATIONS CAN IMPROVE THEIR ETHICS
- Ethics and Compliance Programs — reduce unethical behavior, lower the pressure employees feel
  to cut corners, and encourage safe reporting of misconduct without fear of retaliation.
- Corporate Ethics Officer (a.k.a. corporate compliance officer) — provides leadership on ethical
  issues, ensures procedures are followed, and helps maintain the organization's ethical culture.
- Board of Directors and Ethical Leadership — the board should model honesty and integrity and
  create a safe environment for employees to ask for advice or report misconduct; employees watch
  how leaders behave far more than they read policy manuals.
- Corporate Code of Ethics — a written guide to the organization's values and principles that helps
  employees recognize ethical issues, make sound decisions, and know how to report violations. A
  strong code identifies areas of ethical risk and provides real reporting mechanisms.
- Social Audits — a process where an organization reviews how well it is meeting its ethical and
  social responsibility goals, and sets new goals for the year ahead.
- Ethics Training — reinforces the idea that personal convictions about right and wrong can be
  strengthened through education, not just assumed to already exist.
- Ethical Criteria in Employee Appraisals — evaluating employees not just on productivity, but also
  on how they behave: treating others fairly, operating well in multicultural settings, accepting
  accountability, developing others, and being open and honest with suppliers, customers, and
  coworkers.
- Create an Ethical Work Environment — employees need a safe, known channel for reporting
  concerns. Example: Intel lets employees report suspected violations to management, legal, internal
  audit, or anonymously through an internal ethics website.

ETHICAL DECISION-MAKING MODEL (5 STEPS)
1. Develop a Problem Statement — clearly define the problem, identify who is affected, gather
   facts, and avoid assumptions.
2. Identify Alternatives — brainstorm possible solutions and involve stakeholders where appropriate.
3. Choose an Alternative — weigh solutions by effectiveness, risk, cost, time, laws, policies, and
   the organization's code of ethics.
4. Implement the Decision — communicate, plan, and train as needed to put the chosen solution
   into action.
5. Evaluate the Results — monitor outcomes, check whether goals were met, and adjust if needed.

IT ETHICS TOPICS
The explosive growth of the internet, social networks, and personal data collection has raised the
risk that information technology gets used unethically. Key concerns include:
- Privacy and Security — surveillance and employee monitoring can clash with individual privacy.
- Copyright and Piracy — downloading copyrighted music, movies, or software without authorization.
- Unsolicited Messages — sending unwanted marketing emails and texts (spam).
- Hacking and Identity Theft — stealing personal data from databases and using it fraudulently.
- Plagiarism — using online material without proper credit or attribution.
- Cookies and Spyware — tracking users' online activity and purchases, often without full awareness.

KEY TERMS TO REMEMBER
Ethics, morals, integrity, virtue, vice, law, code of ethics, corporate social responsibility (CSR),
supply chain sustainability, stakeholder, corporate ethics officer, corporate compliance officer,
social audit, problem statement, software piracy, Bathsheba syndrome (the tendency of
successful, powerful people to believe their success entitles them to bend ethical rules).
